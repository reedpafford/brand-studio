## Scope

This skill decides how a House video is shot and cut: where the camera sits, how it moves, how one moment passes to the next, and how a moment is made to land. It never decides what happens in the story, and it never changes how the world looks.

## The camera moves with purpose

The camera is inside the scene and always doing something: a slow drift, a push, a follow. A locked-off frame reads as stiff. One camera move per shot, chosen for what the moment is doing:

- **Push in** on a line that matters or a reaction that matters, so the viewer leans in with the camera. Save it for those moments; a push on everything flattens them.
- **Follow** a character through a space (a waiter weaving through a crowd) at their pace, so the viewer moves with them.
- **Hold still** only for a beat that needs stillness (a stare, a breath before a reveal), and briefly.

## The hook: start close, then reveal

Open on an extreme close-up of the one detail that tells the situation (coins in a wet palm, a cracked phone, a hand frozen mid-reach), then push out or pull back to reveal where we are and who it is. The viewer understands the detail first and the world a second later, which holds them through the first two seconds.

## Emphasis: slow motion, sparingly

Slow motion is for the two or three moments the story turns on (the reveal, the payoff, the one big gag), and only those. Everything around them cuts fast and stays at full speed, so the slow moments land by contrast. A video in slow motion everywhere has no emphasis left.

## Entrances: low angle for a changed hero

When a character returns transformed (new clothes, new confidence, the tables turned), bring them in from a low angle, walking toward the camera at an even, unhurried pace. Their first entrance, before the change, sits at eye level or above, so the low angle reads as the change.

## Mirror shots: setup and payoff in the same frame

When a payoff answers a setup (the same place, the same people, the opposite outcome), shoot the payoff with the setup's framing and angle, so the viewer sees the change at once: the clumsy action and the smooth one, the polite smile and the eager wave, the one who got soaked and the one who gets soaked now. Note the setup's framing when it's planned, so the payoff can match it.

## Transitions: a motivated white flash

A time jump or a change of place passes through a white flash that comes from something in the scene: headlights sweeping the lens, a camera flash, a phone screen's glow filling the frame, a door opening onto bright light. A bare white flash with no source is the fallback, not the habit. Inside one place and time, cut straight.

## Pace

Cuts are quick through setup and montage, and every shot still lands one clear action before it cuts. The pace slows only where the emphasis moments are, and the ending holds long enough for the last line to be heard and read.

## Screens

A screen is always a phone in a character's hand (held up to friends, turned to camera, tapped), never a full-frame insert. When a screen is the point of a moment, push in on the phone in the hand rather than cutting to the screen alone.

## Known limitations & workarounds

- **Slow motion** sometimes comes back at full speed. Name it in the shot as the action ("in slow motion, the drink tips down his shirt") rather than as a camera note, and keep it to the one action.
- **Two moves in one shot** (push in and pan) drift. Pick the one that serves the moment.
- **A mirror shot** only reads if the setup's framing is repeated closely: same side of the subject, same height, same distance.
