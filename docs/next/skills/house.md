## Scope

This skill is a baseline of tools for how a House video is shot and cut: where the camera sits, how it moves, how one moment passes to the next, and how a moment is made to land. None of it is mandatory: use a tool when it serves the story, never by habit, and be creative beyond it. It never decides what happens in the story.

## Light: soft and bright, like the @nateassets reels

House is not a dark film still. Faces are the brightest, softest thing in the frame: soft light from the front or side wraps round them, and the shadow side stays light, warm and full of colour, never grey or black. Shadows are soft and shallow, never hard lines across a face. When the script doesn't set the time, sunny day or golden hour is the easy default; night is kept for the moments that need it (the lonely grind), and even then the face stays lit and clear. A phone's glow is an accent on the hands and a cheek, not a red or green wash over the whole face. Colour is warm and rich but not loud. When a scene's light is written, say it this way ("soft window light on her face, light gentle shadows"), not as high contrast, deep blacks or one hard light.

## Cast who read at a glance

In these shorts every role looks like what it is, the moment it appears, so the story reads on mute: the love interest or the ex is strikingly attractive and glamorous, the rich rival is broad and square-jawed in a sharp suit, the doubting parent looks the part, the underdog hero is likeable and plain at the start and visibly upgraded after the turn. Lean into the type rather than away from it (attractive, never sexual: the video model refuses anything that could read that way). Status lives in props you can see: the ring box, the watch, the car, the phone balance.

## The camera moves with purpose

A held frame with the characters moving is the default; a camera move is the exception, used when it adds something:

- **Push in** on a line that matters or a reaction that matters, so the viewer leans in with the camera. Save it for those moments; a push on everything flattens them.
- **Follow** a character through a space (a waiter weaving through a crowd) at their pace, so the viewer moves with them.
- **Hold still** only for a beat that needs stillness (a stare, a breath before a reveal), and briefly.

## How Nate shoots and cuts (from watching the reels)

Creative direction, not rules. This is what Nate's reels do and ours didn't:

- **The camera mostly holds still; the people move.** Energy comes from the characters: Mom bursts through the door and lunges into the lens, a hoodie is thrust at the camera, a chair spins round. Then a cut, often joined by a quick whip-blur. A camera that pans and drifts around a scene reads as aimless.
- **Close and simple.** Faces fill the frame: nose-to-nose two-shots, a big frontal close-up, an eye. One or two people, an ordinary place (a bedroom, a stock room, a street), soft daylight, muted colour. Not wide shots of small figures in grand sets full of extras.
- **Things make sense in order.** Each shot picks up where the last one left off, and the world keeps its state: the wallet slammed on the desk stays on the desk in every later shot; someone who walks out is gone and the door closes behind them; a spilled drink stays spilled.
- **A scene comes back to the same set-ups.** The same two-shot across the desk, cut against close-ups and inserts, so the viewer always knows where everyone is.
- **Inserts are big and frontal.** A phone held right up to the lens, the number filling the frame, held long enough to read.

## A hook that starts close, then reveals

One strong way in: open on an extreme close-up of the one detail that tells the situation (coins in a wet palm, a cracked phone, a hand frozen mid-reach), then push out or pull back to reveal where we are and who it is. The viewer understands the detail first and the world a second later, which holds them through the first two seconds.

## Emphasis: slow motion, when a moment earns it

Slow motion is a tool for a moment the story turns on (a reveal, a payoff, the one big gag); many videos need none. When it's used, the moments around it stay at full speed, so it lands by contrast: slow motion everywhere leaves no emphasis.

## Entrances: a low angle for a changed hero

When a character returns transformed (new clothes, new confidence, the tables turned), a low angle, walking toward the camera at an even, unhurried pace, can show the change at once, especially when their first entrance sat at eye level or above.

## Mirror shots: setup and payoff in the same frame

When a payoff answers a setup (the same place, the same people, the opposite outcome), shooting the payoff with the setup's framing and angle is a strong option, so the viewer sees the change at once: the clumsy action and the smooth one, the polite smile and the eager wave, the one who got soaked and the one who gets soaked now. Note the setup's framing when it's planned, so the payoff can match it.

## Transitions: a motivated white flash

When a time jump or a change of place passes through a white flash, it reads best when it comes from something in the scene: headlights sweeping the lens, a camera flash, a phone screen's glow filling the frame, a door opening onto bright light. A bare flash with no source is the fallback, not the habit. Inside one place and time, a straight cut usually does.

## Pace: fast, and every cut tells the story

Nate's reels cut fast, often twice a second, and never feel choppy. What they do, as loose guides:

- **Every new shot shows one new thing:** the face the line lands on, the thing it points at, the result. A shot that adds nothing new is the one to drop, not the one to slow down.
- **Both sides of a moment in one shot when they fit:** the insult and the face it hits, the brag and the one who hears it, the hero still visible behind the one talking. A quick exchange can play in one shot with everyone in it; the cut comes for the reaction or the punchline.
- **Movement carries the cut:** a whip, someone walking through, a car pulling away takes the eye into the next shot, so the cuts flow instead of jump.
- **Fast on information, held on feeling:** the turn of the story (hurt hardening into resolve, a slow smug smile) gets a shot that stays while the face changes, then the pace picks up again.
- **Montage shots are tiny before-and-afters:** each one shows an action and its result (the dirty wheel wiped clean in one shot), so even the quickest shot reads as progress.
- **The ending holds** long enough for the last line to be heard and read, and the funniest face (the doubter's scream, the petty grin) can be the last thing we see.

## Screens

A screen is always a phone in a character's hand (held up to friends, turned to camera, tapped), never a full-frame insert. When a screen is the point of a moment, push in on the phone in the hand rather than cutting to the screen alone.

## Known limitations & workarounds

- **Slow motion** sometimes comes back at full speed. Name it in the shot as the action ("in slow motion, the drink tips down his shirt") rather than as a camera note, and keep it to the one action.
- **Two moves in one shot** (push in and pan) drift. Pick the one that serves the moment.
- **A mirror shot** only reads if the setup's framing is repeated closely: same side of the subject, same height, same distance.
