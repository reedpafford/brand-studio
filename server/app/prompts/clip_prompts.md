# Step: Clip prompts
<!-- Inputs: optional <treatment> (the director's, JSON), optional <notes> (the run's notes from people), <video_model> (name, min/max seconds, reference limits, recommended images), <assets> (id, kind, name, what it shows; storyboard frames as frame:<clip id>; voice samples as audio), <breakdown> (JSON), optional <limit> (how long each clip's prompt runs, by its model). Output: ClipPrompts. -->

Write one video prompt per clip in the breakdown. The model generates the whole clip in one go, cuts included, with its own sound, so the prompt is a short production brief for the clip, shot by shot.

**The treatment (<treatment>, when given)** is the director's: each clip plays its beats' pictures with something happening, the first seconds of the first clip are the hook, and the payoff is staged as the treatment says. Within the script's words (see "The editor owns the script", above), the treatment wins over your own staging.

**References.** Use only the assets listed (our own approved character sheets, places, storyboard frames and brand screens; never a raw uploaded photo of a person), never a frame from a reference reel or the house-style board. Always use reference media, never the first-frame setting (the model can't combine the two); the storyboard frame goes in as [Image1] and the References line names it the first frame, which sets the opening composition only: the character sheets set each face, and each outfit unless this video gives them one (their cast line's "wears: … (this video)"), which wins; they override the first frame where they differ, and the References line says so. This video also gives a character an outfit when its story dresses them for its scenes, even when their cast line gives none: the breakdown's action or the treatment puts them in it ("Theo, in his navy wedding suit, waits under the arch"). Name that outfit in the References line ("[Image2] is Theo, in this video's navy wedding suit.") and the Keep line, so the sheet sets the face, hair and build and the story sets the clothes. Name an outfit by its cut, colour and fabric ("a sleeveless champagne satin sheath, high round neck"; the cast line's "wears:" words when it gives them) and write it in the same words in every clip: "a satin dress" alone was drawn with a new neckline in each clip. In order: the clip's storyboard frame, then the characters (the main character's sheet first, the rest in the order they first appear in the clip's shots), the place, then every brand screen the clip's shots list, with the payoff screen last. Keep a clip to 3 screens at most (e.g. a got-rich arc: chart down → chart up → big balance): with more, the model mixes them up. Prefer 8 or fewer images; drop the least important prop or place before a character or a screen. Add a character's voice sample as audio when it has one, and bind it in the References line ("[Image2] is Theo and uses the voice from [Audio1]."). If the assets include a reference video segment for this clip, attach it as video and write "Match the cutting rhythm and camera moves of [Video1]; not its characters, setting or text." Images are [Image1], [Image2], … in the order you list them; videos [Video1], …; audios [Audio1], …

**Prompt shape** (plain text, these lines, nothing else; the first and last lines are the same audio line, word for word):
```
Audio: only the spoken lines and <room sound>. No music, background music, BGM, score, instrumental, melody, synth effects, ambient pad, drone, swelling tone or stinger. No subtitles.
References: [Image1] is the first frame. It sets the opening composition only; the character sheets set each face, and each outfit unless this video gives one, and override the first frame where they differ. [Image2] is Theo. [Image3] is Dad. [Image4] is the <brand> logo: the word <brand> as it's written.
Summary: <who does what, where, and the turn or payoff, in one sentence>.
Style: {{clip_style}}.
Setting: <the scene's look: palette, visible light, depth, background people>.
Shots (hard cuts):
Shot 1 | 0-2 s. Low-angle close-up, push in. Dad leans over the desk, brows crashed down. Dad (in English, shouting): "Seven days. Ten grand."
Shot 2 | 2-3 s. Extreme close-up of Theo, snap zoom: his eyes snap wide. No one speaks.
Shot 3 | 3-4 s. Over-the-shoulder close-up, static. The phone in Theo's hand is turned away from us; its green glow lights his face as a grin breaks across it. No one speaks.
Keep: Theo's identity and clothing stable in every shot, as in [Image2] (red beanie, grey hoodie), and Dad's, as in [Image3] (grey beard, blue work shirt); one of each; Theo never in Dad's work shirt; the <brand> logo as [Image4].
Audio: only the spoken lines and <room sound>. No music, background music, BGM, score, instrumental, melody, synth effects, ambient pad, drone, swelling tone or stinger. No subtitles.
```
Aspect, length and resolution are settings, not prompt words: never write them.

**Concise.** Each prompt runs about the length its <limit> line gives, never past its limit: each shot in few, exact words (an action in general terms, detail only where the moment turns on it, never the same action twice), with the references carrying how the people, places and screens look.

**Nobody speaks unless the script says so.** The model voices anything that reads like a line, so: a clip with no spoken line opens and closes with "Audio: no one speaks (no voices, narration or dialogue), only <room sound>. No music, background music, BGM, score, instrumental, melody, synth effects, ambient pad, drone, swelling tone or stinger. No subtitles." instead; in a dialogue video the model makes that sound itself, so name the sound this moment and the style's guide want under it (the room, and the action's own effects where the guide asks for them: a thump as the bouquet lands, a car door, a ding on a notification), and write "only silence." where the style's guide wants the shot silent; every shot without a line ends with "No one speaks."; nobody adds an exclamation of their own ("Oh my God", "Oh", "Yes!"); and action is what the body does, never a line in disguise (not "does not sell", "says no", "tells her", "replies", "answers", "shouts"; write "types back", "nods", "shakes his head", "his thumb hovers over Sell, then pulls away").

**Montages.** The main character is in every montage shot, in frame from its first moment and already mid-action: write him first ("Theo, already in the doorway, shoves the doors open"), never a reveal, an entrance into an empty frame, or the place before him. Name him in every shot ("Sam's hands fly over the keyboard", not "his hands"). The grind beats before the payoff each get a segment of their own, long enough to read (a late night, a red day, a bigger win), and so does the payoff. One shot is one image: never "two quick cuts" inside a shot, which the model draws as a split screen, and never a split screen. Beats too short for a whole second in one place run on as one continuous action in one shot ("spins in the chair, slams the faders up and leans back, arms wide"); a beat in another place gets its own shot or is dropped. At most one payoff per clip. Each beat is something he does or gets (see the breakdown's montage beats), written as an action with energy, never a pose: he slams, spins, tosses, sprints or laughs, and the camera moves with it. For a trading app, the grind shows him trading, not only reacting: "Sam's thumb scrolls the coin list and taps Buy, his face lit green by the screen". The face comes from his sheet and the outfit from his sheet (or this video's, when it gives one) in every one.

**Opening.** The first shot is the breakdown's first shot, the inciting exchange: the line and the reaction on screen from the first frame (Shot 1 is the close-up of Theo saying "I do." at the altar, and the bride's "HELL NO!" follows in the next second). Never open on a wide establishing shot or a crowd cutaway the breakdown doesn't have.

**Timing.** Shot times are whole seconds written with a plain hyphen ("Shot 2 | 2-3 s."), back to back from 0 to the clip length (the breakdown keeps its decimals; only this prompt rounds). Round where each shot starts and ends, counted from the clip's start, to the nearest second, never each shot's length on its own: rounded lengths add up and slide the later shots off their words. A shot with a line never comes out shorter than the breakdown gives it: where rounding would cut it short, round its end up and take the second from a wordless shot. A shot that shows what a word names starts on or just before that word, never after it. Beats shorter than a second in one place run on as one continuous action in one segment (never "two quick cuts" in one segment), and no segment is shorter than 1 s. A reaction that must be seen gets its own segment, long enough to land, instead of sharing one. In a segment with a line, the line comes at or near its start: the actions the shot also carries play with it or after it, and a setup the beat needs first (rising off the step, a tap on Buy, a long look) is its own short segment, unless the setup is itself the point. Each segment carries only what its seconds can show at phone size, in this style (its guide's own rules win: one motion action a shot where it says so): what the model can't fit, it drops at random, so when a breakdown shot stacks more than its seconds hold, keep the actions the beat needs and let the rest go, and give the cause a change depends on (the elbow that hits the lamp switch before the room turns warm) a segment of its own. The clip's last line never ends in its last moments: leave the closing shot room for the reaction to land, as long as it needs and no longer (never a frozen frame; a held deadpan or comic button where the style's guide uses one), and end on the hand-off the breakdown's `seam` names. The video's last clip ends on one small readable moment (the payoff with its gag or button), landing within about a second of the last word, never a run of actions; the first shot answers the previous clip's hand-off. Spread the dialogue shots across the clip so the talking doesn't finish early and leave dead air.

**Show what a line points at.** When a line names something on a screen or in the room ("That's my face", the coin, the balance), give it its own insert just before or under the line: an extreme close-up of the phone in a hand, upright and filling the frame, the named text big (never a screen pasted in on its own), held long enough to read (about 1.5 s is a good guide). A phone seen over the shoulder is unreadable at phone size. Every result has its cause on screen: a peanut lands on the tray because we see it flicked; a prop moment too short to read is cut, not squeezed in.

**Spoken lines.** Dialogue is `Name (in English, how it's said): "words"`, word for word from the breakdown, and each spoken line appears once in the prompt (a one- or two-word sentence that opens a longer line, like a greeting, gets its own beat in the how-it's-said note, "saying Hey on its own, then a beat": glued to the rest, the model drops it); every line names its language, or the model may speak another one, and a clip with any speech also carries the line "Language: every spoken line is in American English." before its first spoken line. The quoted words are the breakdown's character for character, capitals included (see "The editor owns the script", above); how a name is said goes only in the how-it's-said note, without quote marks (quotes are speech), never into the quote: `Mom (in English, warm, saying <brand> as its notes say): "Get <brand>."`. Every shot with a line says who speaks it and that only their lips move, with every other face in it keeping its mouth closed ("Only Dad speaks; Theo's mouth stays closed."); a listener's shot after a line says their mouth stays closed. A model that voices the lines itself (Omni) cuts the picture on these marks but times the voices by its own delivery, so each line sits wholly inside its speaker's shot, and the next speaker's shot starts only once it has room. Voiceover is `Voiceover (<who>, in English, <how it sounds>): "words"`, and nobody on screen lip-syncs it. On a model that voices the lines itself (Omni), a speaker the treatment's `voices` names gets their voice on its own line before the shots ("Mom's voice: …"), in those words, set once for the whole video: keep that line as it is and don't describe their voice anywhere else (their how-it's-said note is the delivery only). A speaker it doesn't name gets a few words on how their voice sounds in their how-it's-said note, the same words in each clip.

**Making it play.** Acting is specific and sized to the moment: a small, exact gesture (a slow blink, a tight swallow, a hand that stops mid-reach) beats a shock face, and the one big reaction is kept for the turn or the payoff; the winner moves slow and calm, and reactions hold long enough to land. A reveal or a punchline is big, unmistakable and held long enough to read before the reaction; a feeling carries into the next shot, and a call to action after a shock is still said shaken. A sound the body makes (a gasp, a laugh, a scream) is fine when the story motivates it: think about what this moment earns and how it plays for a viewer, write the sound into the shot where it lands, and leave it out where it doesn't (the model voices every sound the prompt names). A reaction names the feeling and its reason, then what the face and body do ("touched by his gift: eyes well, a shaky smile"). A feeling the character hides is never named, only what the face holds: the model acts out a named feeling over the face you write, so a smile held through a humiliation is "smile fixed, eyes flat, jaw tight, a tight swallow, no tears", never "humiliated" or "mortified", which come out as tears. The feeling is a named emotion and its cause, never words a character could say ("can't believe it worked", "here we go"): the video model speaks them aloud, even in a shot with no lines, so gratitude never reads as shock. When a character is refused as a look-alike, change what makes them read as someone famous (the hair: a bun or a ponytail, no bangs), never who they are. Show emotion through face and body, never as light in the eyes; when a feeling runs strong (rage, terror, triumph), add "normal human eyes, never glowing" to the Keep line. In a crying or tearful close-up the face keeps its sheet's age and shape: tears on the same face, never a younger or rounder one. On a payoff line, write the visible end state, never the old problem: if the line says the eyes healed or there's "nothing to wipe", the shot shows his eyes clean, clear and dry, and the prompt never mentions tears, wiping or the symptom (the model draws the words it reads). The Keep line holds each character's identity and clothing across every shot, even when the setting changes: the same face shape, skin tone, age and build as in their sheet, every adult at normal adult scale beside the others and the set, in the official form ("Keep: Theo's identity and clothing stable in every shot, as in [Image2]: the same narrow face, light skin tone, age and slim build"); when this video gives the character their own outfit, the Keep line names it instead of the sheet's ("in this video's mustard trench coat"). The outfit changes only where the script changes it. When a shot's outfit hides a signature feature (a cap over the hair, a hood), keep a cue of it visible and say so ("dark curls showing under the cap"): the model redraws a face it can't recognise. Name each character's fixed look in the Keep line in the words their cast line gives after "name what it shows, in these words only:" (read off their sheet: a dog's breed, coat and marking, e.g. "stocky tan terrier, white chest blaze"; a person's hair, build and skin tone), plus their outfit (this video's when it gives one), so no shot changes their breed or colours. Every character the clip attaches is in its Keep line, by the name its References line gives, with the outfit from their own sheet, unless this video gives them one (their cast line's "wears: … (this video)"), which wins, as does the outfit the story dresses them in for its scenes (see References); never one from the role's or the script's description of who they are (a cast line may still carry an old role's clothes), and that outfit stays the same in every clip of the video unless the script changes it. A character with a sheet looks exactly as it, whatever any words say: never write their face, breed, coat, skin, hair or colouring in a shot or the Keep line beyond those words from their cast line, not even from the breakdown or a role's name ("Pepper, the golden dog" with a French bulldog sheet is "Pepper, as in [Image3]", never "golden coat"). When two or more people share a clip, pin each outfit to its owner and add a "never" for the clothes most likely to swap ("Omar in the grey quarter-zip, never the rival's black leather jacket"): the model otherwise moves one person's clothes onto another. Every action is physically plausible for that body: a dog walks, trots, sits, sniffs, wags; it never leaps straight up, floats or bends wrongly. Mention water, waves or echo only when the scene has them. The camera is always a third-person camera: never a POV shot with the viewer's own hands (a character's hand pushes the door). A shot after a time jump opens on a quick white flash, written first ("White flash. Two years later, Theo…"). One camera move per shot, in standard terms (close-up, push in, pull out, pan, track, orbit, low angle, overhead, handheld); write anything rarer as "term: what you see".

**Never reads as sexual.** The video model refuses a clip that could read as sexual, even an innocent one, and a reworded one still failed: a clip whose first frame or shots show a person in bed, under the covers or undressed carries no bodily sound or action word at all (licking, slurping, sucking, wet, moaning, groaning, panting, gasping, sighing, tongue). The animal's behaviour goes in its own clip with no person in bed in frame ("Pepper licks his own front paw on the rug, close on the paw"); the bed clip only refers to it as "a noise from the foot of the bed", and the person, in pyjamas, reacts with a look (eyes open, pulls the pillow over her head), never a sound.

**Brand screens.** Any shot with a screen shows it on the phone (or monitor) in the scene, in a character's hand or in front of him, over his shoulder or close on his hand holding it, with his face or hands in frame; never a screenshot filling the frame flat-on. The phone is held steady and angled to the camera, close enough that the brand's logo reads, and shows [ImageN], named by its screen every time, without quote marks (e.g. "shows [Image5], the <brand> Home (rich) screen"; quotes mean speech), with its UI, logo, colours and layout exactly. Then name the coin, numbers or chart the shot needs, if any ("shows [Image5], the <brand> Token chart up screen, a coin up to +$4,200"): the screen's look comes from the reference, its numbers from the story, so no two shots copy one screenshot's coin and price. Never describe the screen's look in your own words. Never invent a screen or a figure: a moment the brand has no screen for is shown another way, the phone turned away from us with its light on the face (red for a loss, green for a win) and the reaction carrying it. On a real screen, name the chart's shape and its one number, and make each winning beat bigger than the last and visibly rising (+$40, then +$900, then +$12,000). Always name the brand's word with the logo (the word <brand> as it's written): the picture alone came out misspelt or as the icon with no word. Play a screen beat for drama: his face lit by the screen's glow, then a cut over his shoulder onto the phone in his hand, his thumb tapping; never a phone held up flat to us, except at the end line or a camera swap the script calls for. Keep glare off the display, and fingers off the logo. A got-rich payoff shows a big portfolio balance (hundreds of thousands), never a coin's price: use the listed screen that already shows one (its name says rich, portfolio or balance), else the home screen, and name the balance. The end line's shot (the call to action, "Want to trade like me?") is the one where the screen faces the camera: the character holds the phone up to the lens beside his face, in the scene, steady and large in frame, so the brand's logo and the app as it really looks read clearly ("Omar holds his phone up to the camera beside his grin; it shows [Image4], the <brand> Home (rich) screen, the <brand> logo sharp and legible"). Any of the brand's home-type screens will do; prefer the one that already shows a big balance. A camera swap or flip the breakdown calls for is written as one: "Whip pan into a front-facing selfie angle: Omar holds the phone at arm's length and talks straight to the lens; his other hand holds up [Image4], the <brand> Home screen", never a plain cut to him holding the phone up. The brand's product appears only as its attached [ImageN], never an invented bag or label of your own. A product (a bag) is shown as [ImageN]: its shape, logo and main text exactly, no face or limbs unless the script makes it a character. It's a prop in the action, used the way the story uses it. On a product moment (a shot whose `moment` is the product's, a ★ line), its front label faces the camera, upright and steady; never ask for a label to camera on a pack being tipped, poured or shaken (it comes out upside down): show the label before the pour, or the pour from the bowl's side with the pack's top edge in frame. In any other shot, name only where it is and what's done with it, with no ask for its label. When the label faces the camera, frame the product no tighter than the whole bag, with its main text large: the brand name, the main claim line and the flavour ("PEANUT BUTTER FLAVOR") must read; small print may blur, so never a close-up of a badge, the net weight or the fine print. No captions, subtitles, titles or overlay text. Text in the world (a 3D number, a sign, a label, a scoreboard, a screen) appears only when the script or the shot calls for it, its words stated exactly; never random or made-up writing, which comes out garbled.
