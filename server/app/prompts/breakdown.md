# Step: Breakdown
<!-- Inputs: <brief>, <cast>, <places>, <video_model> (name, min/max seconds per clip, reference limits), <script> numbered, optional <reference> (a reel this video adapts), optional <recent_payoffs> (where the brand's latest videos' montages landed, one video a line). Output: Breakdown. -->

Turn the working script into scenes, shots and clips that the team will storyboard and generate. The editor can edit everything afterwards; give them a breakdown that already plays well.

**Shots.** Cover every script line, in order, with no gaps. Lines that start with a tag in brackets ("[Camera] …", "[Movement] …", "[Reaction] …", "[Brand screen] ★ …") are directions the editor accepted: use them to shape the shot they sit next to and list their line numbers in that shot's `script_lines`; they are never spoken or shown as text. The words in `line` are the script's, unchanged. Break dialogue so each speaker's line and each turn in an argument gets its own shot (shot and counter-shot), and give the big moments their own reaction shot; a reaction names the feeling and its reason, then what the face and body do ("touched, can't believe she's being kind to him: eyes well, a shaky smile"), so a gasp of gratitude never reads as shock. The house style (the most-viewed animated reels) cuts about once a second overall, with a median shot of about 0.55 s: arguments and reactions 0.3–1 s, wordless montage shots about 0.4 s, nothing over 3 s except a dialogue two-shot or one continuous reveal move; the low point is the one place it slows (a 3–5 s held close-up with a slow push in). Every big line cuts to the listener's face. Inserts (the phone in his hand, the number, hands on the keyboard, the clock) move the story without dialogue. Use standard camera terms (close-up, push in, pull out, pan, track, orbit, low angle, overhead, handheld; anything rarer as "term: what you see"), and write camera so it carries emotion: push in on the doubter's accusation, a high or isolated wide for the low point, a low angle and a tracking move for the payoff.

**Voiceover.** For a voiceover script, `speaker` is the narrator (e.g. 'Dog (V.O.)') and `line` holds the exact words heard during that shot; split long sentences across shots so the picture changes every 1–3 s and always shows what the words say. A dog narrator's mouth doesn't move unless the script makes the dog talk on screen.

**Scenes.** One per place or time. The `look` tells the story state in light and colour: cold blue and dark for struggle, the warm glow of a laptop or phone lighting the face for the grind, warm gold or neon for the win, background people who react when it's public.

**Brand.** Tag each shot's `moment` from the brand block. Any shot whose moment has a screen gets that screen in `screen_ids`, and the reaction to it in the next shot. The screen is always on the phone (or monitor) in the scene, in the hand of the character it belongs to or one he's looking at: over his shoulder, or a close-up of his hand holding the phone, with his face or hands in frame, so list him in `character_ids`. Never a screenshot filling the frame flat-on, and never a phone held up flat to the viewer. Play a phone beat for drama, in two cuts when there's room: his face lit by the screen's glow (the chart's green or red on his skin and in his eyes), then over his shoulder onto the phone in his hand, his thumb on it. Frame it close enough that the fomo logo (or the brand's name) reads; its numbers can be anything. The end line's shot (the call to action, e.g. "Download fomo through the link in my bio") is the one shot where the screen faces the camera: the hero holds his phone up to the lens beside his face, in the scene, so the brand's logo and the app as it really looks read clearly; list him and that screen (the one with the big balance when the brand has it, else the home screen). If a trading or brand moment has no ★ line in the script, still show the screen: it's required. The video ends on about 2 s of the real brand element (the app screen on the phone he holds up, the bag) when the script gives it a closing line.

**Montages.** A montage is a run of quick wordless shots. The main character is in every one of them, in frame from the first moment: no empty rooms, no establishing shots without him, no entrance into an empty frame. An insert of his hands, phone, screen or keys is his shot: list him in its `character_ids` and name him in the action ("Theo's hands fly over the keyboard"). Never another character's hands or a helper's trade on its own: if a beat shows someone else, the hero is in frame with them. When growing wealth matters to the story, every beat reads as trading → getting rich: concrete, escalating payoffs he's part of, cut against the brand where it fits (the trade on his phone, the desk screen). Invent the payoffs for this story and these people: what this hero wanted, what he lost, who doubted him (a teen who'd be bagging groceries gets his own studio and courtside seats; a jilted groom buys the beach house above the shore where he was left). Car keys, a new car, a penthouse and a balcony are one such run among many, not a template: never the same run as the <reference>'s montage. When there's a <recent_payoffs>, the payoffs are new against those too: never a moment, place or thing one of the brand's recent videos already landed on. The got-rich payoff shows a big portfolio balance (hundreds of thousands), never a coin's price, on the screen that already shows one when the brand has it (its name says rich, portfolio or balance), else the home screen; the screen's numbers can be anything, its logo and layout can't. Any clip uses only the screens its beats need, 3 at most (for a got-rich arc: chart down → chart up → the screen with the big balance): no home or app-open insert before the trade, and no desktop screen unless the script asks for it. The video model mixes up screens when a clip carries more. **The arc.** One climb with one dip: for an app brand (fomo), the offer of a way out cuts straight to someone opening the app on the phone with the others leaning in, intrigued; then grind beats with small wins that each look bigger, one tense moment, then the payoff. Celebrate only at the payoff, never mid-grind. **Earn the payoff.** Before the got-rich run, the montage grinds: at least three effort or setback beats that build, e.g. a red day he shakes off, a small first win, another late night, a bigger win. Each grind beat holds about 0.8 s so it reads, and it can be his face, his hands, the room or the clock moving on, not only a screen (the 3-screen limit per clip still holds). **Show the trading.** For a trading app (fomo), the grind is him trading, in the app: his thumb scrolls the coin list and picks one, taps Buy, drags the amount, leans in as the chart ticks. Side business between result screens (notes, spilled food) never stands in for it. Then the payoff run cuts fast (about 0.4 s a beat). Make every beat an action, not a pose: he does something with energy (slams the laptop shut, sprints up the stairs, tosses the keys, spins in the chair) with a camera move that sells it. He keeps one outfit through the montage unless the script changes it: a new place for each payoff, never a new costume. Never jump from one trade straight to the payoff. When there's a <reference>, the montage runs about as long as its montage (the <reference> gives the seconds), give or take a fifth: never squeeze it into one short clip. **Money climbs.** Every trading or win insert that shows money states, in its action, the figure the screen reads ("the position reads +$42 (+3.1%)", then "the balance reads $1,240", then "$18,900"), and each is bigger than the one before, so the wins read at a glance. A red day may show a loss; after it the figures climb on from where they were. Keep grind figures well under the payoff (under $100,000). The got-rich payoff states a clearly large balance, six figures or more and bigger than every earlier figure (any number that reads as wealth, e.g. "the balance reads $512,400"). These figures tell the story in the script; the screen itself only has to show the brand's logo right.

**Clips.** Group consecutive shots into clips the video model generates in one go, each within its min and max seconds. A clip holds one scene: always end it where the place or time changes, never carry it across a scene heading. Outside a montage, a clip runs at most about 12 s and 12 shots, because faces drift in longer, busier clips: split a longer scene at a natural cut (on a reaction or between two lines, never inside a line). A montage keeps its quick cuts within the model's limits. With voiceover, end clips between sentences. A clip that starts after a time jump opens on an insert (phone, screen, hands) before the hero's face.

**The hook is the inciting moment.** When the script opens on an exchange (a proposal and the no, a dare, a sale), the first shots stage exactly that: the line and the reaction to it, clearly on screen. No establishing shot, cutaway, crowd reaction or insert comes before it.

**Reference.** When there is a <reference>, match it beat by beat: its shot lengths and cut rhythm, its framing and camera moves, how big the acting is, and its light and palette, unless the script or brand needs something else. A section marked "Replace" keeps its length and its device (e.g. match cuts: same pose, bigger world) with the brand's content and screens in it. Live-action segments are gone; their slot is the brand's end line over the real brand element.

Use only ids from the user message and the brand block.
