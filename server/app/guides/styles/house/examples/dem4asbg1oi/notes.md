## 5. Pacing
- Overall 9.1 cuts per 10 s, but the rhythm changes by segment (kit numbers; segment = shots starting inside it):
  - **Cold open dialogue (0-6.5 s):** 5 shots, 0.6-1.83 s, median 1.3 s.
  - **Flashback inserts and memory (6.5-15.2 s):** 11 shots, 0.30-2.57 s, median 0.47 s, 9 under 1 s. This is the fastest CG stretch: three 0.3-0.47 s inserts, then one 2.6 s hold for the emotional moment.
  - **Time card:** 0.73 s.
  - **Present-day dialogue (15.9-36.4 s):** 10 shots (about 12 with the hidden cuts), 0.77-3.87 s, median 1.83 s, 1 under 1 s. Slower: two-shots and close-ups held through a line, with the longest hold (3.87 s, side-on two-shot) on a pleading exchange and a 1.5 s silent stare.

- Cuts and words: 6 of 26 CG cuts land mid-word (the picture cuts to a listener while the line carries on). The rest fall in gaps between lines.
- Cuts and beats: 8 of 26 CG cuts are within 80 ms of a beat, about what chance gives (27%), so CG cuts follow the dialogue, not the music. In the tag, 4 of 9 are on a beat, a bit above chance. *(Inferred: the tag is cut loosely to the drop.)*

## 6. Camera
- **Mostly static or slow.** Per second, 15 of 39 s read as static or near-static, and most of the rest is a slow push in (2-14%/s) or a small pan. Only two CG moments move fast: the push on her walk-away (shot 5) and the pan on the opening door (shot 25).
- The "handheld shake" the numbers report in CG shots (shots 10, 18, 20, 23, 26) is, in the strips, smooth character motion, hair and hugs, plus a gentle drift. **No CG shot is genuinely handheld.** Real handheld shake belongs only to the live-action tag (shake up to 64).
- Eye level throughout the CG, with one low, from-behind walk-away (shot 16). Over-the-shoulder framing is used constantly: a foreground shoulder, sunglasses, hair or bouquet sits soft at the frame edge (shots 2, 3, 4, 14, 22, 24, 25).
- Shallow depth of field everywhere: backgrounds become soft bokeh (crowds, flashguns, city lights), and foreground objects (bouquet, glasses) are blurred.
- No whip-pans inside the CG. The fast "whip" smear transitions are only in the live-action tag.

## 7. Transitions
- Hard cuts are the default.

- Present-day jump: a short fade to black, then a **black card with a serif time label** (0.73 s), then a hard cut.
- CG to live action: a hard cut, then whip-smear cuts between phone clips.

## 9. Lighting
- **Golden-hour backlight** is the signature in the cold open and the rooftop: a low sun behind the cast, rim light on hair, long shadows toward the camera, peach-pink skies and lens bloom.
- **Night scenes** go to deep blue (sky and ambient) with a warm key on faces and warm practical lamps behind (balcony, shots 21-24). This is where the guide's warm-against-cool-blue look shows most clearly.
- **Memory scenes** are over-exposed and bloomed: blown sun haze (shot 7), white-washed hug (shots 11-12). The kitchen is dim and low-contrast.
- One daylight scene is bright and blue-skied (13-16), and the office reveal ends in cool daylight (end of shot 26, shot 27): lighting changes between scenes instead of holding one time of day.
- Highlights do blow out in the memory and flash moments, on purpose.

## 16. Style pattern (what makes it House)
1. Stylised 3D feature-animation cast (huge expressive eyes, sculpted brows, glossy blushed skin) in real-world, densely dressed places with lifelike props.
2. Motivated cinematic light that changes per scene: golden-hour backlight with rim and long shadows; night scenes in deep blue with warm faces and warm practicals; memories bloomed and washed out.
3. Eye-level, mostly static or slow-push camera; lots of over-the-shoulder framing with soft foreground edges; shallow depth of field with bokeh crowds and lights.
4. Dialogue scenes held 1-4 s per shot, broken by bursts of 0.3-0.5 s inserts for time jumps.
5. Time jumps marked by a motivated white flash or bloom, and a black serif time card.
6. Outfits carry the transformation: plain tee and cap become designer, glasses and ice.
7. Edited on top: yellow word-by-word captions in the lower middle, a constant loud music bed with sound-design hits, and a live-action "real life" tag that hard-cuts in on a beat drop.

## 17. Why it works (look and feel)
- 0-2 s: a bright, beautiful golden-hour wide with a character walking in gives instant place and warmth. The first cut comes at 1.8 s and the second at 3.1 s, so the eye gets a new face every 1-1.3 s.
- Big readable faces in close-up (shots 3, 4, 19, 22, 24) carry the emotion even with the sound off. The captions carry the words.
- The flashback burst (three 0.3-0.47 s inserts, bleached memory light) reads instantly as "the past" without explanation.
- The serif black card and the sudden switch to luxury sunset settings sell the time jump and the change in status at a glance.
- Long holds in the confrontation (3.9 s two-shot, 1.5 s stare) let the payoff land. The live-action tag then breaks the fiction on a beat drop.

## 18. How the director recreates this look from a new script
- Keep: the stylised 3D cast in real places; motivated light that suits each scene (golden hour, warm-on-blue night, bloomed memory); eye-level framing with over-the-shoulder edges and shallow focus; 1-2 s dialogue shots with a 3-4 s hold on the key exchange; quick 0.3-0.5 s inserts only for time jumps or montage; a white-flash or serif-card time jump; outfits that show change.
- Don't copy: the specific places (graduation, penthouse); the designer logos (the LV-style cap and branded bags are another brand's marks); the live-action creator tag (that's the creator's own footage, a format choice, not a look the model makes); gibberish full-frame UI (use the brand's real screens); the gown and colour continuity slips.
