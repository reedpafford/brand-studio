## 9. The style pattern (look, light, colour, camera)
- **Render**: stylised 3D feature-animation CG. Big glossy eyes, thick sculpted brows, soft skin, simplified noses and chins; clothing with real cloth folds; props and environments close to photoreal (bin-bag plastic, cardboard, wet asphalt, warehouse racking).
- **Talent**: the teen hero keeps one outfit in every shot (navy cap worn backwards, white tee with a small dark chest logo, thin silver chain, black trousers, white trainers, stud earring). The adult antagonist is a caricature: tall, very broad square jaw, navy suit and tie, slicked hair, heavy brow; reaction close-ups push his face to extreme cartoon (saucer eyes, giant teeth). Background workers are generic and closer to realistic.
- **Render drift**: no-character inserts and crowd wides (packaged stock, the watch tray, the warehouse floor in 34-36) come out near-photoreal; the characters stay stylised. The mix is part of the look of these reels *(inferred: likely a generation artefact, not a choice; flag, don't prescribe)*.
- **Settings**: real, everyday places, densely dressed: a stockroom walled with stock, a suburban front door at dusk, a park, a rainy residential street with lit windows, a small box room under a ceiling panel light, a big distribution warehouse with racking and conveyors, loading bays in late sun.
- **Light**: motivated and changes with the story. Low point = night: screen glow under the face, phone glow on hands (reddish), rain, cool blue darkness with warm amber windows. Hope/work = day: flat bright daylight, blue sky. Success = cool-neutral high-key warehouse skylights. Dusk doorstep = warm amber door light against blue-grey. Deep blacks, no blown highlights except deliberate flash blooms.

## 10. Why it works (look and feel, second by second)
- 0-5 s: one held wide sets place, power (the adult towers) and stakes at a glance; dark light makes it feel hostile; the per-word captions carry it with sound off.
- 5-11 s: the cut to a warm, sad dusk doorstep and a nose-to-nose profile shot escalate without new places to learn.
- 11-18 s: the montage turns failure into rhythm: same frame, new garment, every 0.2-0.5 s, with a click or whoosh on each cut.
- 18-28 s: the one long, cold, rainy shot is the emotional floor; then time jumps inside it and the crash zoom into the eye marks the turn.
- 31-43 s: daylight arrives with the change of fortune; flash-framed "bigger pile" match cuts and sped-up taping make growth visible without words.
- 43-54 s: a slow, sunlit arrival and a door that opens on a vast bright warehouse give the payoff scale; the cartoon reaction close-up is the laugh; the hero turning to the lens closes it.
- 54.5-56 s: a fast real-world flicker with a music drop changes the register and loops.

## 11. How the director recreates this look from a new script
- Keep: one locked or near-locked frame per dialogue exchange (1-5 s holds), with the camera at or just below eye level; montages of 0.2-0.6 s shots built on one repeated pose or framing that changes one thing per cut; a single long emotional-low shot; motivated transitions (a flash, a door, a wall of objects, a crash zoom).
- Keep the light arc: night/screen-glow/cool for the low point, warm amber practicals at dusk, bright daylight or high-key interiors for the win.
- Keep the hero's single outfit and silhouette identical in every shot; keep antagonists more caricatured than the hero.
- Keep captions as an editing pass: per-word, bold italic yellow with black outline, about 60% height.
- Sound for the editor: a low drone bed under everything, a whoosh/click/impact on montage cuts, ambient effects that sell the place (rain, notifications, a horn), a music drop only for a closing tag.
- Don't copy: the creator's real-life tag, the specific merchandise, the title line wording, the near-photoreal inserts (a drift, not a target), the full-frame laptop screen inserts if the House guide's "phone in hand" rule stands.
