## Zoom-in behaviour (the measurement Reed asked for)
- **How often:** the camera pushes in on 24 of 45 shots (53%), 62% of the runtime (deep-watch: zoom above
  1.5 %/s). Another 9 shots creep in at 0.9–1.5 %/s, so **33 of 45 shots (73%) drift inward** to some extent.
  Only 1 shot drifts out (−0.7 %/s), and nothing pulls out on purpose. A push starts about every 3.8 s
  (2.4 pushes per 10 s). Runs of consecutive pushing shots last 1–5 shots.
- **How fast:** almost all pushes are slow and steady: median **2.0 %/s**, range 1.6–3.6 %/s for character,
  body close-up and macro shots (about 4–8% tighter by the end of a 2–3 s shot). Only two fast moves:
  shot 22 (+14.6 %/s, a fly along an artery right after the cut inside) and shot 44 (+30.6 %/s, a dolly along a
  storage shelf). The calendar inserts are locked off (0 %/s).
- **Profile:** the push runs from the first to the last frame of the shot at one constant speed. No ease-in, no
  zoom that starts or snaps on a word. My second meter agrees on the shape (constant small positive steps) and
  on the two fast moves (+24 %/s and +48 %/s by scale-search; it reads higher because cells and parallax move toward
  the lens).
- **On what:** in character shots, the push is centred on the skeleton's head and torso (it ends a little tighter on
  the face's expression). In body close-ups it pushes onto the glass calves and the fluid in them. In macro shots it
  travels down or into the vessel. The product shots push on the jar or pack.
- **"Showing what is said" happens by cutting, not by one long zoom.** When the voice names a body part or
  process, the edit cuts to a dedicated close-up (the glass calves: shots 1, 5, 13, 20, 24, 27, 36) or straight
  inside the body to a 3D medical macro (shots 22–25, 29, 32–35). There is no continuous zoom from a wide shot
  into the body; the slow push simply keeps every shot alive.
- **Camera or edit?** *(inferred)* The pushes are generated camera moves, not a digital scale added in the edit:
  shot 7 and shot 44 show parallax (the background lamp and the shelf posts shift against the subject), and the
  frames stay sharp at the end of each push. Captions sit on top and don't scale.
- **Timing against words and beats:** cuts land within 0.1 s of a word start 55% of the time, against 59% for
  random times, so they're not tied to words. 12 of 44 cuts fall mid-word, and caption pages run across cuts.
  Cuts on "beats": 30% against 45% random. There's no music, so the "beats" are voice syllables. Since every push
  starts at a cut, the pushes aren't timed to words or beats either. The edit follows the meaning of the line (a
  new picture per phrase), not the rhythm.

## Style pattern
- **The character:** a cute, toy-like 3D skeleton. Oversized smooth round skull, huge glossy blue cartoon eyes
  with eyelids (they blink, roll and widen), small grin of teeth, slim ivory bones with smooth plastic-toy
  surfaces. Its torso and calves are wrapped in a **clear glass/plastic shell**: a see-through jacket shape with a
  collar over the ribcage, and bulb-shaped glass calves that hold amber fluid. The level and glow of that fluid
  are how the body's state is shown. No organs, no skin, no hair. The same design in every shot.
- **Settings:** warm, lived-in, photoreal-styled 3D homes: bedrooms with quilts, living rooms with bookshelves
  and rugs, kitchens with wood counters, a white-tiled bathroom. Practical table lamps and window light.
  Shallow depth of field and soft bokeh. It's a feature-animation render of a cosy home, not a pastel studio
  backdrop.
- **Inserts:** (1) a desk tear-off calendar flipping its page (the time-ladder device), warm lamp-lit;
  (2) 3D medical macros of vessels with red cells, yellow fatty build-up, grey calcium, amber droplets: glossy,
  textbook-illustration style; (3) glowing amber abstracts (a molecule, a heart) on dark backgrounds;
  (4) product and ingredient still lifes in a cellar and on wooden shelves.
- **Light and colour:** warm amber-gold throughout. Per-shot averages: brightness 10–64 (median 47), contrast
  13–29, saturation 9–68 (median 51), warmth +4 to +43 (median +21; every shot is warm). Character shots median
  brightness 48, warmth +19. Calendar shots about #95704D, warmth +28. Macros are the warmest and most saturated
  (median warmth +30, saturation 56; e.g. #C65D4C, #B66652). Product shots are darker (median brightness 29;
  the cellar shot falls to brightness 10). Sampled frames: 13 s #886649, 24 s #94643D, 38 s #947C65, 50 s #87524A,
  82 s #3F2F25.
- **Camera:** eye-level or slightly low, mostly medium and medium-wide on the character, centred. Every shot
  holds still except a slow push in. Two fast moves in 99 s.

## Lane match
- **No current lane fits it exactly; it's a hybrid.**
  - The character matches **skeleton-cute** (Skeleton Mascot): oversized round skull, big adorable eyes, smooth
    toy-like bones, friendly and non-scary. But its proportions are taller and leaner than "a small rounded
    body".
  - The places do **not** match skeleton-cute's "simple clean pastel background, soft even studio light". They
    match the default Bare-Bones `place_look`: a rich, warm, softly hazy home with shallow depth of field.
  - The see-through shell that shows a body state (fluid in the calves) is the **skeleton-xray** idea, "the
    inside shows what the line says" in plain visual words, without x-ray's blue glow and red organs.
  - The 3D medical macro inserts aren't covered by any lane.
- So: the Mascot character in the Bare-Bones world, with an x-ray-style "see the inside" device and
  medical-macro cutaways.

## Why it works (for the viewer, look-wise)
- 0–2 s: an odd, satisfying physical picture (glass legs draining) with no face to read first. You look to
  work out what you're seeing.
- The calendar flip is a clear visual "next step" every 6–10 s, so the ladder reads with the sound off.
- The glass calves make an invisible body state visible and measurable: full, glowing, draining, empty. Each
  close-up is the proof of the line.
- Cuts inside the body to glossy macros give a "this is what's really happening" moment and a jump in colour
  and saturation that resets attention about every 10–15 s in the middle.
- The cute character keeps a medical subject light and non-gross. Its eyes (sleepy, wide, surprised) carry the
  feeling of each rung.
- Constant slow pushes keep every frame alive without calling attention to the camera.

## How the director recreates this look from any script
- One cute toy skeleton (round skull, big blue cartoon eyes) with a **see-through shell over the body part the
  story is about**. Show the state with something inside that fills, glows, drains or clears, not with organs
  or gore.
- Put it in warm, cinematic, cosy real-world rooms with practical lamps and window light; shallow depth of field.
- Per line: a medium shot of the character acting the line, or a close-up of the shelled body part, or (when the
  line explains a mechanism) a 3D medical macro inside the body. Use about 4 body close-ups and 9 macros per
  100 s, and hard cuts only.
- Mark each rung of a time ladder with a 0.7–1.1 s locked insert of a single, repeated counter object (here a
  desk calendar flipping its page).
- Camera: a slow constant push in on almost every shot (about 1.5–3.5 %/s, start to end of the shot). Save one or
  two fast fly-ins (15–30 %/s) for the first cut inside the body and for a reveal near the end.
- Shot length 0.7–6 s, median about 1.8 s, about 4.5 cuts per 10 s.
- Sound: one narrator, near wall to wall, no music. *(inferred)* That is this video's choice; the house might
  add a bed.
- Captions in the edit: ALL CAPS 2–4 word pages, white, yellow active word, at about 68% height.
- **Don't copy:** the product, its pack and label, the specific ingredients and the cellar/warehouse still
  lifes (they belong to this brand); the exact anatomy story (legs and vessels); the calendar if the script's
  ladder isn't about days.
