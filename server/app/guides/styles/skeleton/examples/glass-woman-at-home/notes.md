## Zoom-in behaviour
- **How often:**
  - 37 of 60 shots push in (62%). They cover 66% of the runtime.
  - 67% of all seconds have zoom above 1.5 %/s.
  - About 3.2 push shots per 10 s; a new push starts every 2.4 s (median gap).
  - Pull-outs are rare: 1 shot.
  - Runs of consecutive pushing shots: 2, 3, 1, 1, 4, 6, 5, 1, 3, 1, 5, 5. The middle (50–75 s) and the close (104–116 s) are almost all push.
- **How fast:**
  - Almost all pushes are a slow, even creep from the first frame to the cut: median 5.3 %/s (range 1.6–13.3).
  - My meter gives totals of x1.05–x1.53 per shot, with steady steps of +1 to +3.5% per 0.2 s.
  - There is no snap zoom. The quickest (shot 33) gains about 10% of scale, mostly in its first 0.4 s.
- **Speed by subject:**

  Product shots push hardest for their length: x1.30–1.39 in about 2 s.
- **On what:**
  - Character shots push toward the body part being talked about: the belly (9, 19), the face and cheeks (25, 26, 58), the torso's visible organs (8, 27).
  - Macro shots push into the substance (fat clusters, clot).
  - Product shots push into the label.
- **Showing what is said is done mostly by cutting, not zooming.** Each new line gets a new picture of that thing, already framed on it: a belly close-up, a face close-up, or a cut straight inside the body (8 macro or graphic inserts, 16 s, 14% of the runtime). The slow push then adds pressure inside the shot.
- **How it's made:**
  - The pushes look like generated camera moves, not a scale added in the edit, because the product shots and shots 27 and 28 show parallax. That part is my reading, not measured.
  - Captions stay the same size through every push, so they are an overlay.
  - I found no punch-in cut (the same image re-cut tighter).
- **Timing:**
  - 78% of cuts land within 0.1 s of a word starting, against 63% for random times. Cuts sit on word onsets, and since a push runs the whole shot, each push starts on a word.
  - Only 24% of cuts land on a beat, against 39% for random times. Cuts are not timed to the music.
  - 11 of 59 cuts fall mid-word.

## On-screen text (an editor layer, never in the footage)
- **Words per caption:** one word at a time, swapped on each spoken word; occasionally a 2–3-word page.
- **Look:** heavy rounded geometric sans (Poppins or Montserrat ExtraBold class), ALL CAPS, white, with a dark soft drop shadow and thin dark edge. No highlight colour.
- **Position:** centred at about 66% of frame height, over the belly or pelvis in character shots; occasionally shifted left or right.
- **Animation:** each word pops in at about 110–130% of its size for one frame (about 0.1 s), then settles. Some long or key words stay larger.
- **Size:** about 25–35% of frame width for a typical word.
- **Other on-screen words:** the product label is legible in pack shots. There are no other title cards and no number cards: the Day markers are pictures, not text, apart from the captions.

## Style pattern
- **The character:** one recurring glass-body woman, the same in every character shot.
  - A clear glass or plastic body shell with an ivory, slightly stylised skeleton inside.
  - Visible organs: pink lungs, brown liver, beige coiled gut.
  - A cartoon skull face with large round eyes, eyelids and lashes in the sockets, and long wavy brown hair.
  - Outfits change per scene (sports bra and leggings, jeans, black leggings) but sit inside or over the shell.
  - The problem shows as substance inside the body: olive-brown sludge, gold blobs, yellow fat, gold lines. It spreads or clears to show the change.
  - Expressions are broad cartoon acting: eyes popping, worried brow, hands to face.
- **Places:**
  - Photoreal, lived-in American homes: bathroom vanity, bedroom with an unmade bed, walk-in closet, hallway with wood floors, living room.
  - Plus two plain white "studio" rooms (38, 43–44) used to show the whole body's state clearly.
  - Soft window light or warm sconces, shallow depth of field.
- **Macro inserts:** glossy, wet, high-detail 3D medical cutaways (tubes and vessels cut open, fat clusters, glowing fluid) on dark or blurred organic backgrounds. They are more saturated and warmer than the home shots.
- **Late inserts:** real-photo ingredient close-ups, one stock-style DNA graphic, live-action UGC (a real woman, real hands with bottles) and a photoreal lab. The look gets less animated as the video moves to the product. *(Inferred: borrowed stock and UGC.)*
- **Colour:**
  - Warm, neutral beige grade.
  - Median warmth +12 (10th–90th percentile +5 to +19), brightness 47 (37–63), contrast 19 (17–22), saturation 31 (14–45).
  - Character-shot averages cluster around #6d5b4b–#8e7c6e (warm taupe). The top of the frame samples #79654e and #776c5d.
  - Macros run to #926841 and #b49385, with saturation up to 63; the DNA graphic is #822d3c.
  - The lab and white-room shots are the only cool-neutral ones (warmth 3 to −9).
- **Camera:** eye level, mostly frontal, centred subject. The slow push is the default move; a few shots are locked off, one tracks back, one is handheld UGC. No pans to speak of.

## Lane match
- **No lane matches exactly.** This is a hybrid: Dressed skeleton + X-ray organs, in Bare-Bones warm photoreal homes.
- **Against skeleton-xray:** it shares the "show what's happening inside the body" idea and the visible organs, but has no blue-tinted skin, no glow, no blue backdrop and no sci-fi medical look. The shell is clear and neutral, and the organs are natural pink and brown, not lit red and orange.
- **Against Bare-Bones (default):** it shares the warm photoreal home places, shallow depth of field and the lovable cartoon-eyed ivory skeleton, but the body is not bare: it has a glass shell, organs, long hair and outfits.
- **Against skeleton-cute:** no chibi proportions and no pastel studio.

## Why it works (for the viewer, look-wise)
- **Frame one:** a cute but slightly uncanny see-through woman holding a gross mass in her belly. The problem reads with the sound off.
- **Every 1–3 s:** a new picture of exactly the thing named (the belly, the face, the inside of a vessel), so the eye always has something new to check.
- **The constant slow push** keeps even static acting shots moving and adds quiet tension. Nothing ever settles.
- **The inside-the-body macros** are the payoff images: gross-satisfying glossy sludge breaking up and flushing.
- **The ending moves to real people and real hands,** borrowing UGC trust after the cartoon explanation.

## How the director recreates this look from any script
- **The character:** cast one recurring glass-body character (clear shell, ivory skeleton, organs visible, a cartoon skull face with big eyes, one identifying trait such as hair) and dress them for each scene. Show the problem as substance inside the shell, the same colour every time, and let it grow or clear across the video.
- **One picture per spoken idea:**
  - When the line names a body part, frame on it (a close-up on the belly or face).
  - When it names a process inside the body, cut to a glossy macro cutaway of that process.
  - Change the picture on the word, not on the beat.
- **Camera:** a slow push on about 60% of shots, 2–8 %/s (x1.05–x1.5 over the shot), running from the first frame to the cut. Push harder (about 12 %/s) on product shots. No transitions: hard cuts only.
- **Places:** warm photoreal home rooms, plus a plain white room when the whole body's state must read clearly.
- **Captions:** an editor layer of one word at a time, white heavy caps with a shadow and a pop-in, at about two-thirds of frame height.
- **Sound:** wall-to-wall narration with a low pad and sub-bass bed; the bed drops out for the product close.
- **Don't copy:** the product, its label and pack shots, the ingredient plants, the stock DNA graphic, the lab and the real-person UGC clips. Those belong to this brand's close. The specific body story (what the substance is, and the Day markers) is also this script's, not the style's.
